If you manage an office, a medical suite or a small building, you probably carry a ring of keys that gets heavier every year. One for the front door, one for the back, one for the supply closet, one for each private office. Meanwhile, nobody is quite sure who else has copies.

A master key system fixes both problems. You carry one key that opens everything you are responsible for. Your staff carry keys that open only the doors they need. Here's how it works, in plain language.

## The basic idea

In a master key system, every lock still has its own key. The difference is that the locks are set up so a second key, the master, opens them too.

A typical small office might look like this:

- **Change keys** (sometimes called individual keys) open one door or one group of doors. The receptionist's key opens the front door. A dental hygienist's key opens the front door and the staff room.
- **The master key** opens every door in that group. The office manager carries this one.
- **A grand master key** sits one level higher and opens everything under several masters. In a building with several tenants, the property manager carries the grand master, each tenant gets a master for their suite, and employees get change keys.

You don't need all three levels. Many offices in Walnut Creek, Concord and Pleasant Hill only need two: one master for the manager and individual keys for everyone else.

## How one lock can take two different keys

Most commercial door locks use small spring-loaded pins inside the cylinder. The right key lifts each pin to exactly the right height, and the lock turns.

In a master keyed lock, a locksmith adds a tiny extra pin, called a master pin, in some of the pin stacks. That creates a second height where the lock will also turn. One height matches the change key. The other matches the master key. The locks are pinned so each one answers to its own key and to the master, and nothing else.

That's why a master key system is planned on paper first. Every lock and every key has to fit into the plan, or you end up with keys that open doors they shouldn't.

## What you get out of it

- **Fewer keys.** Managers carry one key instead of a ring.
- **Clear access by role.** Staff open what they need. The server room, the records room and the cash office stay limited to the people you choose.
- **Faster changes.** When someone leaves or a lock is compromised, a locksmith can rekey just the affected locks and keep the rest of the system working.
- **A record.** A good system comes with a key schedule: a list of which key opens which door and who has each key.

## Key control matters more than the stamp

A lot of businesses rely on "Do Not Duplicate" stamped on the key. Many hardware stores and kiosks copy them anyway.

If you want real control over copies, ask about a restricted keyway. With a restricted key system, blanks are not sold to the public, and new keys are cut only through the locksmith for an authorized person on your account. We set up restricted key systems on Primus and Mul-T-Lock keyways. That's what makes a master key system hold up over years of staff turnover.

Either way, keep a key log. Write down every key handed out, the date, and the person. When someone leaves, you know exactly which key to collect, and if it doesn't come back, exactly which locks to rekey. Our post on [rekeying when an employee leaves](/blog/rekey-business-when-employee-leaves) covers that part.

## Signs your building is ready for one

- You have more than a handful of doors and more than a handful of people with keys.
- Managers carry a ring of keys and still borrow one now and then.
- You don't know how many copies of the front door key are out there.
- You've had to rekey the whole building after one lost key.
- You're moving into a new space, renovating, or adding a suite.

## Master keys or key fobs?

It doesn't have to be one or the other. Many of our commercial customers put a key fob or keycard reader on the main entry and the doors that see the most traffic, then use a master key system for interior offices, closets and storage. The fob doors give you a log of who came in and let you shut off a lost fob in seconds. The keyed doors stay simple and cheap to maintain. ## FAQ

**Is a master key system less secure than individual keys?** A master key system adds one more key that opens each lock, so the master has to be tightly controlled. Paired with a restricted keyway such as Primus or Mul-T-Lock and a key log, most businesses end up with better control than they had before, because they finally know who has what.

**Can my existing locks be master keyed?** Often, yes. If the cylinders are in good condition and the same type, they can usually be rekeyed into a master key system. Worn or mixed hardware may need to be replaced. We'll tell you which after the walkthrough.

**What happens if someone loses the master key?** The locks that master opens should be rekeyed. That's one reason we keep the number of master keys small and recommend a restricted key system.

**Can a master key system grow with my business?** Yes, if it's planned that way. We leave room in the key plan for new doors, new suites and new levels.

**Do I need a master key system if I already have key fobs?** Many buildings use both. Fobs on the main doors, master keyed locks on interior doors and storage. The key system also gives you a backup way in if the power or the access system is down.
